\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Modern communication, sensing, and microwave systems impose increasingly
demanding requirements on the semiconductor devices that form their
front-end signal paths. Higher operating frequencies, tighter noise
constraints, and strict linearity targets continue to push transistor
technologies beyond the capabilities of many silicon-based platforms.
\cite{chaturvedi2021} Although silicon processes have made notable progress
in recent years, they often fall short in providing the necessary electron
mobility, noise performance, or high-frequency gain required for
performance-critical microwave and millimetre-wave applications
(e.g.\ silicon's 1200~cm\textsuperscript{2}/Vs compared to GaAs's
6500~cm\textsuperscript{2}/Vs in transistors for MMICs). \cite{chaturvedi2021}

III--V High--Electron--Mobility Transistors (HEMTs) play a central role in
addressing these challenges. Their heterostructure-based design creates a
highly conductive channel at the interface between two semiconductor layers
with different bandgaps. At this interface a two-dimensional electron gas
(2DEG) forms, supporting exceptionally high carrier mobility. The resulting
strong transconductance, stable high-frequency behaviour, and low-noise
performance have firmly established HEMTs as core devices throughout
microwave and millimetre-wave circuit design. \cite{mishra2002}

Research activity within the field has increasingly shifted toward gallium
nitride (GaN) HEMTs, driven by the material properties enabled by its wide
bandgap. GaN supports high breakdown voltages, strong power-handling
capability, and robust operation under large electric fields, making it the
preferred choice for many high-power and high-linearity applications.
\cite{Mishra2008} Despite this growing dominance, gallium arsenide (GaAs)
HEMTs remain one of the most mature and well-characterised III--V transistor
platforms. Their stability, extensive documentation, and long industrial
history continue to make them an invaluable system for studying the
relationships between heterostructure design, device architecture, and
electrical behaviour. \cite{Mimura2002,Mimura1980} For the purposes of this project, 
GaAs presents clear practical and technical advantages. 
The Leeds Nanotechnology Cleanroom maintains established, reliable processes 
for GaAs/AlGaAs heterostructures, and suitable wafers are readily available 
with reproducible epitaxial quality. \cite{leedscleanroom}

Standard GaAs/AlGaAs HEMTs are inherently depletion-mode devices (d-mode);
they naturally conduct current at zero gate bias and require a negative
voltage to switch off. \cite{Mimura1980} This `normally-on' behaviour is
often undesirable as it necessitates complex negative-polarity power
supplies \cite{st_d_mode} and complicates the design of direct-coupled
logic circuits. \cite{Xie2011} Consequently, there is significant interest
in developing enhancement-mode devices (e-mode) that are `normally-off'
and offer reduced power consumption.

One common approach to achieving e-mode operation is to recess the gate
into the AlGaAs donor layer, thereby reducing the charge carriers available
to form the channel and shifting the threshold voltage in the positive
direction. \cite{Saitto2006} Achieving e-mode operation through gate
recessing is inherently non-trivial: it requires precise and repeatable
removal of only a few nanometres of material, with small variations in etch
depth leading to significant changes in threshold voltage and device
behaviour. \cite{Lanford2005}

The primary objective of this project is to investigate the relationship
between gate recess depth and threshold voltage in GaAs/AlGaAs HEMTs, with
the aim of achieving enhancement-mode operation, and with a focus on
understanding the practical limitations and repeatability of the fabrication
process. This was investigated through the development and iterative
refinement of a gate recession process in which the gate contact is recessed
into the AlGaAs donor layer to shift the threshold voltage into the positive
region.

The work proceeds in two phases. The first phase established a validated
depletion-mode baseline: a full set of unrecessed GaAs/AlGaAs HEMT devices
was fabricated and electrically characterised, confirming that threshold
voltage is governed by the vertical heterostructure and is independent of
lateral device geometry. This baseline provides the reference against which
all subsequent recessed devices are compared. The second phase developed a controlled gate recess etch process through
three successive device fabrication iterations, each designed to diagnose
and address specific failure modes identified in the previous run, including
bypass current paths, mask design limitations, over-etching, and
gate-semiconductor interface degradation.

\section*{Report Organisation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Report Organisation}

Chapter~\ref{ch:technical-background} reviews the device physics of GaAs/AlGaAs HEMTs
and establishes the analytical relationship between donor-layer thickness,
the quantum-mechanical correction to the gate-to-channel separation, and
threshold voltage. This framework is then used to predict the critical
recess depth required for enhancement-mode operation in the specific
heterostructure employed in this work. Chapters~\ref{ch:baseline-process}
and~\ref{ch:baseline-electrical} document the baseline depletion-mode fabrication
process and its electrical characterisation respectively, establishing the
reference dataset against which all recessed devices are compared.
Chapter~\ref{ch:gate-recess-process} develops and calibrates the gate recess
etch process, including its chemistry, oxide-mitigation strategy, and the
mask architecture used to sweep recess depth across multiple devices.
Chapters~\ref{ch:iteration-1},~\ref{ch:iteration-2}, and~\ref{ch:iteration-3} each present a
successive fabrication iteration; each was motivated by a specific failure
mode identified in its predecessor, and together they map the practical
boundaries of the process. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} consolidates these
outcomes into a broader failure analysis and discussion of process
limitations, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} draws conclusions and
recommends directions for future work.
