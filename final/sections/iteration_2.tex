\chapter{Enhancement-Mode Iteration 2}
\label{ch:iteration-2}

\section{Iteration 2 Design Changes}

The failure analysis from the first enhancement-mode devices showed that the recessed-gate
geometry did not force the measured source--drain current through the
recessed region. The second iteration was therefore designed to address this lateral
geometry limitation directly.

\vspace{1mm}

The first design change was to adjust the recess mask. Instead of using two
small trenches, the revised mask placed
the recess beneath the full gate area. This was intended to remove
the unrecessed bypass path identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:iteration-1}, so that
all source--drain current would pass through the recessed area.

\vspace{1mm}

A second change was made to the gate geometry itself. In the previous design,
part of the narrow mesa section between drain and source was not necessarily
fully intersected by the gate, meaning that some of the conducting path could
remain weakly modulated. The gate in this iteration was therefore redesigned so
that it cut completely across the mesa to intersect the source and drain. This
geometry was intended to improve electrostatic control and allow the gate to
fully pinch off the channel in the unrecessed control devices.

\section{Electrical Results}

The electrical measurements showed a clear difference between the unrecessed
control devices and the recessed devices. The unrecessed controls retained
normal depletion-mode HEMT behaviour, whereas the recessed devices did not
function as transistors. This showed that the revised geometry allowed the
recessed region to dominate the measured transistor characteristics.

\vspace{1mm}

\subsection{Recessed Device Response}

Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-recessed-control-transfer} compares the mean
gate-transfer response of the recessed devices with the unrecessed control devices.
The recessed devices remain close to zero drain current across the measured
gate-voltage range, indicating that the source--drain channel was no longer
conducting after the recess process.

\vspace{30mm}

Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-gate-geometry-pinch-off} demonstrates the effect of
the gate-geometry change by comparing the unrecessed controls from Iteration 1
and Iteration 2. Since neither device was recessed, the difference in $I_D$ can
be attributed to the revised gate structure. The original gate design still
conducts at approximately \SI{0.2}{\milli\ampere} when biased below $V_{th}$,
whereas the Iteration 2 control falls to approximately
\SI{0}{\milli\ampere}. The below-threshold sweep in
Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-gate-geometry-pinch-off-below-vth} makes this
difference clearer, confirming that extending the gate across the mesa improved
electrostatic control and allowed full pinch-off.

  unrecessed controls.}
  \label{fig:iteration2-gate-leakage}

Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-gate-leakage} shows an additional effect of the
recessed structure. The unrecessed control devices remain close to zero gate
leakage current across the measured $V_{DS}$ range, whereas the recessed
devices show a clear increase in negative gate current as the magnitude of the
applied gate bias is increased. This behaviour was theorised earlier in
Chapter~\ref{ch:technical-background}: recessing changes the gate interface and
reduces the effective gate-to-channel separation, increasing the likelihood of
gate-current conduction through the modified barrier. This figure demonstrates
that effect in a measured gate-recessed HEMT.

\vspace{1mm}

The failure of the recessed Iteration 2 devices was therefore attributed
to the recess etch process rather than the mask geometry. The geometry change
solved the bypass-current problem identified in Iteration 1, but the recessed channel
was no longer able to support drain--source conduction. This points to
excessive removal or damage of the active heterostructure beneath the gate
region, which is examined further in the failure analysis.

\section{Failure Analysis}

The electrical results indicate that the primary failure mechanism in
Iteration 2 was excessive recess depth. Unlike Iteration 1, the revised mask
geometry no longer allowed the drain--source current to bypass a local recess
trench. As a result, if the recess etch removed too much of the active
heterostructure, the measured transistor behaviour would be lost completely.
This is consistent with the recessed devices remaining non-conducting during
electrical testing.

\vspace{1mm}

The recess depth was not measured using the AlphaStep during this fabrication
run. Instead, the decision was made to assess the recess either electrically or
by direct SEM inspection. Once the devices were found to be non-functional, the
electrical response could only show that the channel had been removed or
damaged; it could not determine the physical etch depth. An SEM cross-section
was therefore the most direct method for identifying how far the recess had
etched into the material stack.

Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-recess-sem} gives evidence of an etch depth of
approximately \SI{150}{\nano\metre}. This is far beyond the intended recess
depth and explains why the recessed devices no longer operated as transistors.
At this depth, the etch would be expected to remove the donor and channel
region required for drain--source conduction, rather than only shifting the
threshold voltage towards enhancement-mode operation.

\vspace{1mm}

This result once again highlighted the sensitivity of the wet etch process, but
it also revealed a key limitation in the etch curves developed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:gate-recess-process}. The calibration was based on incremental
etches, with depth measurements taken between steps, whereas the device recess
was etched continuously.

This difference, combined with variations in etchant mixing, agitation, timing,
solution condition, exposed area, and sample handling, can significantly change
the effective etch rate. The Iteration 2 result therefore showed that the
calibrated etch curves were not necessarily transferable directly to the device
without additional process control.

\vspace{1mm}

The gate leakage observed in Figure~\ref{fig:iteration2-gate-leakage} was not
the cause of the non-functional recessed devices, but it confirms an
important trade-off in the recess process. Moving the gate closer to the channel
can improve electrostatic control, but it also increases the sensitivity of the
device to gate leakage current.

\vspace{1mm}

The combined diagnosis is therefore that the mask redesign worked, but the
recess-depth control did not. The new gate geometry improved pinch-off in the
unrecessed control devices, confirming stronger electrostatic control of the
mesa. However, the recessed devices failed because the etch was too aggressive,
removing too much of the active material beneath the gate region.
