\chapter{Exploration of Enhancement-Mode HEMT Fabrication (Semester~2 Aims)}

\section{Experimental Objective}

Building on the theoretical framework established in Chapter~2 and the
baseline depletion-mode devices fabricated and characterised in
Chapters~3 and~4, the aim of the second phase of this project is to
experimentally investigate the relationship between gate recess depth
and threshold voltage. Chapter~4 demonstrated that threshold voltage is
independent of lateral device geometry, confirming that
threshold-voltage engineering must be achieved through controlled
modification of the vertical electrostatics beneath the gate.

\vspace{2mm}

To isolate this dependence, a set of devices will be fabricated in
which all physical parameters are held constant, with the exception of
the gate recess depth being varied across devices. This controlled
approach enables an empirical relationship between gate recession and
threshold voltage to be established.

\section{Gate Recession Process and Etch Technique Selection}

The fabrication workflow described in Chapter~3 is retained without
modification, with the exception of the introduction of a gate recess
etching step prior to Schottky gate metallisation. This additional etch
locally thins the AlGaAs donor layer beneath the gate, reducing the
effective gate-to-channel separation and modifying the electrostatic
condition required to fully deplete the two-dimensional electron gas at
$V_G = 0\,\mathrm{V}$.

\vspace{2mm}

Several etching techniques may be employed to implement this recess,
most notably dry plasma etching and wet chemical etching. Dry etching
offers superior depth control and repeatability; \todobib however, it
introduces increased process complexity, longer development times, and
the risk of plasma-induced surface damage, which may degrade the
gate--semiconductor interface and complicate interpretation of
electrical results. \todobib Given the exploratory and depth-swept
nature of this study, an etch process providing a wide and forgiving
process window is preferred.

\vspace{2mm}

For this reason, a wet chemical etch was selected. In particular, a
dilute sulphuric acid based solution was chosen to achieve relatively
slow and controllable etch rates, ensuring that nanometre-scale timing
errors do not translate directly into large variations in recess depth
and threshold voltage. The selected chemistry is a
H$_2$SO$_4$ : H$_2$O$_2$ : H$_2$O mixture in the ratio 1 : 1 : 250, which
is reported in the literature to provide low etch rates in both GaAs
and AlGaAs, making it well suited to time-controlled recessing of the
donor layer.

\vspace{2mm}

Preliminary etch trials were conducted using this solution to verify
its suitability for controlled gate recession. Measured etch rates of
$0.373\,\mathrm{nm\,s^{-1}}$ in GaAs and
$0.161\,\mathrm{nm\,s^{-1}}$ in AlGaAs were obtained. These values are
in close agreement with representative literature data for the same
etch chemistry, as summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:gate_recess_etch_rates}. The close correspondence,
particularly for AlGaAs, demonstrates that this dilute etch provides a
stable and predictable process window appropriate for systematic
variation of recess depth.

Although the measured GaAs etch rate is modestly lower than the
literature value, this is plausibly attributable to surface oxides at the beginning of the etch.
Crucially, the slow etch rates and strong GaAs to AlGaAs selectivity provide sufficient
control to enable reproducible, time-based definition of recess depth,
making this chemistry well suited to an empirical investigation of
threshold-voltage sensitivity.

\subsection{Surface Oxidation Issues and Mitigation}

A key challenge associated with wet etching of the AlGaAs donor layer
is rapid surface oxidation. Aluminium-rich layers readily form stable
native oxides upon exposure to air \todobib, leading to non-linear etch
behaviour (Section~\ref{subsection:AlGaAs_oxidation}) and, if left
unaddressed, degradation of the Schottky gate interface. This issue is
particularly critical for enhancement-mode devices, where
nanometre-scale variations in the effective gate-to-channel separation
can produce large shifts in threshold voltage.

To mitigate these effects, a method involving a brief hydrochloric
acid (HCl) dip immediately prior to gate metallisation will be explored
in Semester~2 as a means of removing the native oxide layer. Minimising
the delay between oxide removal and gate metal deposition is expected
to suppress re-oxidation and improve reproducibility of gate behaviour
across the device set.

\section{Experiment and Device Mask Design}

\subsection{Mask Design Rationale}

In the device mask, eight distinct sets of devices will be fabricated
with targeted gate recess depths ranging from 10~nm to 45~nm in
increments of 5~nm. This range spans the full thickness of the donor
layer, from shallow recesses expected to have minimal influence on
threshold voltage by removing only the GaAs cap, to deeper recesses
anticipated to induce enhancement-mode behaviour. Literature reviewed
in Chapter~2 suggests that normally-off operation should emerge once
approximately 25~nm of the donor layer has been removed; this informs
the decision to extend the depth sweep beyond this threshold.

\vspace{2mm}

The devices will be produced across two separate samples processed in
parallel, with each sample divided into four quadrants. Within each
quadrant, five recessed devices share a common target recess depth,
alongside an unrecessed control device. The control devices provide an
internal reference against the depletion-mode baseline established in
Chapter~4, allowing unintended global process variations to be
identified during electrical characterisation.

\vspace{2mm}

\vspace{2mm}

Across the two samples, recess depths are interleaved between
quadrants. This layout ensures that, in the event of partial sample
failure or localised processing defects, data spanning the full range
of recess depths may still be obtained. The quadrant-based organisation
also simplifies device identification during probing and enables
straightforward correlation between electrical behaviour and intended
recess depth.

\subsection{KLayout Implementation and Fabrication Implications}

The mask was implemented in KLayout, following the same design rules as
the depletion-mode devices, with the addition of a dedicated gate
recess etch layer. Figure~\ref{fig:enhancement_mode_device} shows the
complete device layout and a magnified view of the gate region,
highlighting the recess mask relative to the Schottky gate.

\vspace{2mm}

The mask layout directly informs the fabrication timeline. Each sample
requires four lithography and etching steps to realise the full set of
recess depths, with the two samples processed in parallel to maximise
cleanroom efficiency. By encoding the experimental design into the
mask itself, the transition from fabrication to characterisation is
simplified, and the risk of ambiguity in recess depth assignment is
minimised. This mask therefore represents the final preparatory stage
of Semester~1, providing a robust and experimentally efficient
framework for enhancement-mode fabrication and characterisation during
Semester~2.

\section{Semester~2 Fabrication Timeline}

The Semester~2 fabrication and characterisation work is planned around
one full cleanroom session per week. The timeline prioritises
completion of a robust enhancement-mode device set using a controlled
gate recess process, with stretch objectives included where time and
process stability permit. The core sequence is designed to complete in
seven cleanroom days, leaving margin for minor rework or iteration.

\vspace{2mm}

Figure~\ref{fig:semester2_gantt} presents a Gantt-style overview of the
planned workflow. The sequence follows the standard device process
established earlier in the project, with additional sessions allocated
to recess calibration and quadrant-based gate recessing. Electrical
testing and characterisation are included as core activities, while
wire bonding, packaging, and scanning electron microscopy are treated
as stretch goals contingent on progress.
