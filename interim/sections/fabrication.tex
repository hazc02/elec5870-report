\chapter{Fabrication of Depletion-Mode HEMT Devices}

\section{Overview of the Fabrication Workflow}

The depletion-mode HEMTs characterised in this report were fabricated using 
the standard III-V device process established within the Leeds Nanotechnology 
Cleanroom. This workflow follows the routine sequence used for 
GaAs/AlGaAs transistors in this facility: it consists of 3 main stages: mesa definition, 
ohmic contact formation, and gate formation. 
This well-established procedure was followed without modification and carried 
out over four full-day laboratory sessions. The devices produced 
through this workflow provide a necessary baseline; they allow for a reliable 
comparison against which the behaviour of e-mode devices with different structures 
and dimensions can be evaluated later in this project.

\section{Mesa Definition and Isolation Etch}

The fabrication process began with the electrical isolation of individual 
device regions through mesa etching. Prior to lithography, the 
GaAs wafer was cleaned sequentially in acetone, isopropanol (IPA), and 
deionised water (DI) to remove organic residues and ensure sufficient 
photoresist adhesion. To further enhance the bonding interface 
between the substrate and the resist, a Surpass primer was spun onto the 
wafer. This priming step is critical for wet-etching applications: it 
modifies the surface tension to prevent the etchant from penetrating under 
the resist edges which would otherwise 
lead to the delamination of the mask. A positive photoresist (S1803) was 
then spun onto the sample at 3000\,rpm, soft-baked on a hot-plate at 115\,$^{\circ}$C, 
and subsequently patterned with exposure from the Heidelberg MLA 150 
Maskless Aligner (MLA) \cite{leedscleanroom_equipment}.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]

  \vspace{3pt}
  \caption{Mesa lithography mask used for defining isolated device regions (Post-Development).}
  \label{fig:mesa_mask}

Following exposure, the sample was immersed in solution of MF-351 developer diluted with DI (Ratio 1 : 3.5)
for 45 seconds, followed by a thorough two-stage deionised (DI) water 
rinse and nitrogen blow-dry to reveal the mesa patterns. The mesa pattern was 
then transferred into the wafer using a wet-etch solution of sulphuric acid, 
hydrogen peroxide, and water ($H_2SO_4$:$H_2O_2$:$H_2O$ in a 1:8:160 ratio).

\vspace{1mm}

This etchant is well-suited for GaAs/AlGaAs mesa formation, as it provides a 
controllable, moderately fast isotropic etch that removes the active layers 
uniformly~\cite{notten1991mechanism}. The etch rate is slow enough for precise 
timing of a shallow $\approx$200 nm recess, yet fast enough for routine device 
isolation.

\vspace{1mm}

To calibrate the process, an initial 30-second pilot etch was performed. 
Subsequent measurement with an Alpha-Step profiler showed an etch depth of 
114 nm, indicating an initial rate of 3.8 nm/s. To achieve the target depth of 
200 nm, an additional 86 nm of material needed removal. Assuming a constant 
etch rate, a second etch of 22 seconds was calculated and carried out. However, 
the final measured depth was only 177 nm, meaning the second etch removed just 
63 nm at a reduced effective rate of approximately 2.9 nm/s. Although 177 nm is 
adequate for device isolation, successfully penetrating the active GaAs/AlGaAs 
heterostructure layers, the rate discrepancy underscores the sensitivity of the 
wet-etch chemistry.

\vspace{1mm}

\subsection{Analysis of Etch Rate Discrepancy}
The main hypothesis for the reduced average etch rate in the second step is the 
formation of a native oxide layer on the GaAs surface during the interval when 
the sample was removed for profilometry. This oxide creates an ``incubation 
period'' at the start of the second etch, delaying material removal while the 
etchant penetrates the oxide to reach the underlying semiconductor. Since the 
second etch (22 seconds) was shorter than the first (30 seconds), this 
non-productive period accounted for a larger fraction of the total time, 
lowering the overall average rate.

\vspace{1mm}

A more specific attribution points to rapid oxidation of the AlGaAs donor layer 
surface during the profilometry interval. Unlike pure GaAs, which forms 
relatively unstable oxides, AlGaAs reacts with atmospheric oxygen to create a 
robust native oxide layer primarily composed of thermodynamically stable 
aluminium oxide (Al$_2$O$_3$), along with Ga$_2$O$_3$ and 
As$_2$O$_3$~\cite{passlack1997thermodynamic, bessolov1998chalcogenide}.

\vspace{2mm}

The $H_2SO_4$:$H_2O_2$:$H_2O$ wet-etch mechanism involves a continuous cycle: 
surface oxidation by the peroxide, followed by acid dissolution of the resulting 
oxide~\cite{notten1991mechanism}. However, the air-grown, Al-rich passivation 
layer dissolves much more slowly than the transient, Al-poor oxides formed in 
situ during steady-state etching~\cite{bessolov1998chalcogenide}. As a result, 
the second etch step includes a substantial incubation period to breach this 
stable Al$_2$O$_3$ cap before resuming the intrinsic etch rate of 3.8 nm/s. In 
a brief 22-second etch, this nonlinear delay constitutes a significant portion 
of the process, reducing the calculated average rate.

\section{Ohmic Contact Formation}

The formation of low-resistance Ohmic contacts is a critical prerequisite for 
accessing the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) within the heterostructure. 
When metal is deposited directly onto the GaAs cap, the interface naturally 
forms a Schottky barrier due to Fermi-level pinning at the surface states: 
this creates a rectifying junction that restricts carrier injection. To 
overcome this, a specific metallisation scheme and thermal processing step are 
required to facilitate field-emission (tunnelling) conduction. The objective 
is to create a heavily doped $n^{+}$ region beneath the contact pads that 
extends down towards the channel; this high doping density significantly 
narrows the depletion width of the barrier, allowing electrons to tunnel 
freely between the metal and the semiconductor with linear $I-V$ 
characteristics.

\vspace{1mm}

To define these contacts, a dual-layer photoresist stack comprising 
Lift-Off-Resist (LOR) beneath S1803 was employed. A single photoresist 
layer typically develops near-vertical sidewalls: this causes the evaporated 
metal to form a continuous film across both the wafer surface and the 
photoresist edges, making lift-off unreliable and often leaving metallic 
"fences" around the contact perimeter. The dual-layer stack mitigates this by 
exploiting the higher developer solubility of the LOR base layer. During 
development, the LOR recedes laterally more than the S1803 above it, 
producing a distinct undercut profile. This physical overhang breaks the 
continuity of the deposited metal film, ensuring that the metal on top of 
the photoresist is electrically and mechanically separated from the device 
contacts. Consequently, solvent-based lift-off yields sharply defined 
openings free of edge defects.

\vspace{1mm}

\input{figures/fig-lift-off-stack.tex}

\vspace{1mm}

Following lithography, the Ohmic contacts were formed by thermally evaporating 
a AuGeNi eutectic pellet. This alloy is commonly used for n-type 
GaAs Ohmic contacts because each element plays a specific role during annealing. 
Germanium diffuses into the GaAs and acts as a donor, creating a highly doped 
region beneath the contact. Nickel improves the uniformity and adhesion of the 
metal film and supports the diffusion process, whilst gold provides a low-resistance 
conduction path between the contact pad and the semiconductor.

\vspace{1mm}

To activate the contacts, the sample underwent Rapid Thermal Annealing (RTA). 
It is during this thermal cycle that the transition from rectifying to Ohmic 
behaviour occurs. The heat drives the Germanium atoms to diffuse through the 
AlGaAs layers and into the GaAs lattice, locally creating the 
$n^{+}$ doped region. This effectively "thins" the potential barrier at 
the interface, establishing a low-resistance tunnelling path that connects 
the drain and source electrically to the 2DEG.

\section{Gate Formation and Final Inspection}

With the Ohmic contacts established and annealed, a third photolithography 
step was performed to define the Schottky gate region. As with the previous stage, a 
dual-layer resist stack was utilised to ensure a clean lift-off. The gate 
electrode was aligned centrally between the source and drain contacts; its 
placement is critical, as it provides the electrostatic control required to 
modulate the carrier density within the channel.

\vspace{1mm}

Following development, a Ti/Au stack was evaporated to form the gate 
electrode. In this configuration, the Titanium (Ti) layer serves as a vital 
adhesion layer to the semiconductor surface, while the thick Gold (Au) layer 
provides a stable Schottky barrier and minimises gate resistance. Unlike the 
Ohmic contacts, the gate metallisation is not annealed; preserving the 
Schottky barrier height is essential for minimising leakage current and 
maintaining effective depletion control over the channel.

\vspace{1mm}

After metallisation, the resist was removed using solvent lift-off to reveal 
the final device structure. A small-number of lift-off defects were observed under the microscope (Figure~\ref{fig:poor_lift_off}) and 
subsequently removed using brief, gentle ultrasonic cleaning. The removal of 
these artefacts is essential for device reliability: they can otherwise cause 
electrical short circuits, particularly around the sensitive gate edges.

\vspace{2mm}

\vspace{2mm}

The completed devices were examined using optical microscopy to ensure process 
integrity. The mesas exhibited clean, well-defined sidewalls; the Ohmic pads 
were properly positioned; and the gate electrodes showed sharp, uninterrupted 
edges with no evidence of bridging or residual flakes. Overall, the 
fabrication process yielded a high-quality array of depletion-mode HEMTs 
suitable for subsequent electrical characterisation.

\section{Summary of Fabricated Device Set}

The fabrication process yielded a structured array
of depletion-mode GaAs/AlGaAs HEMT devices arranged in a grid across
the wafer. This enabled multiple device geometries to be fabricated
simultaneously under identical process conditions, facilitating systematic
electrical characterisation while minimising process-induced variation.

\vspace{2mm}

In total, fifteen HEMT devices were fabricated and subsequently characterised.
The array comprises five distinct columns, each containing three nominally
identical devices. Each column corresponds to a specific lateral device
geometry defined by a combination of gate length and source--drain separation, outlined in Table~{\ref{tab:device_geometries}}.

The three nominally identical devices within each column provide a direct
measure of device-to-device variability and fabrication repeatability for a
given geometry. This device set therefore establishes a well-controlled
baseline. The electrical characterisation presented in Chapter~4 uses this
baseline to assess depletion-mode device behaviour and to validate the
unrecessed fabrication process as a reference for subsequent enhancement-mode
development via controlled gate recession.
